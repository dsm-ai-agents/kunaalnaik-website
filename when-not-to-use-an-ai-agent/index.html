Agent selection

Most work should not be an agent.

A selection guide, and a deliberate counterweight to agent enthusiasm. The fastest way to waste money on AI is to build an autonomous workflow for work that should have stayed manual or become a simple script.

The problem this solves

Agent capability is being sold as universally applicable. It is not. Agents are expensive to build, easy to break, and require ongoing maintenance — costs that only pay back for a specific class of work.

What the engagement should produce

  • A test for process stability: unstable processes must not be automated first
  • A reversibility check: irreversible actions need a human, not a gate you can skip
  • A frequency threshold below which automation never repays its build cost
  • An ownership requirement: no named owner means no agent
  • A cheaper-alternative check: a script, a template, or a form often wins

Ways to work together

  • Selection advisory session
  • Workflow triage review
  • Portfolio prioritisation workshop
  • Second opinion on a proposed build

Who it is for

Leaders and teams holding a list of automation candidates who want the list shortened honestly before budget is committed.

No black-box transformation. The goal is internal capability, explicit ownership, and evidence that the workflow improved the work.

How scope is decided

Send the audience, workflow or business priority, current tools, constraints, and desired outcome. The first response will identify the smallest useful starting point and any missing information—not force a standard package.

Common questions

Before you enquire.

Is this really a service?

It is the first hour of most consulting engagements, and it saves the most money. Deciding not to build is a legitimate and frequently correct outcome.

What should we use instead?

Often a template, a form, a database constraint, or a twenty-line script. The review names the cheapest thing that solves the actual problem.
